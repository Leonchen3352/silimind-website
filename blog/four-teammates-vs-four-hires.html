Four AI teammates vs four hires: the seat-vs-salary math

2026-11-22 SiliMind

Most B2B teams hit the same wall somewhere around their fourth hire. The first person covers the phone and the inbox. The second takes quotes and follow-ups. The third starts producing content, because someone has to. Then the work outgrows all three again, and you end up staring at a spreadsheet trying to answer a question that has no clean answer: hire another person, or buy something?

This piece does not settle that with a slogan. It lays out the arithmetic you can actually do, marks the parts where only you have the missing number, and says out loud where the math stops being honest.

What this is not

Three disclaimers up front, because they shape everything below.

It is not an argument for replacing anyone. The workflow we build assumes the opposite: AI handles the chores, the human makes the calls. Every row in the table below ends with a human decision.

It is not a price list. We don’t put our own pricing in this article. Where a number is needed, you supply it. If you want to know what our side costs, that’s a conversation, not a blog post.

Nor is it a savings percentage. There is no “cut your costs 40%” conclusion here. Part 3 explains why such a number would be made up.

What it is: a fill-in-the-blanks template, plus the benchmarks you need to fill it honestly.

2. Your seat cost

Take the per-seat monthly price you already pay your CRM and multiply by the number of seats. That’s a number you have today, and it belongs in the table whether you hire or not.

3. On-costs, with a range not a figure

Employer contributions, equipment and desk space vary by city and policy. Put a range in, note the assumption, and move on. A precise-looking number here would be false precision.

Part 2 · What needs exactly one number from you

This is the part most cost articles fudge, so we’ll do the opposite: the formula is below, and the inputs are yours.

People cost depends on your market, seniority and whether you count on-costs.

Part 3 · What we won’t pretend to compute

Three items, and the reasons they stay outside the table.

“AI is X% cheaper than hiring.” No. The two sides don’t do the same job. A hire negotiates, reads a room, decides when to walk away. An AI teammate drafts, summarises, checks, reminds. Comparing their costs per unit requires pretending they’re substitutable, and they aren’t.

Payback period. “It pays for itself in N months” is an ROI multiple with a calendar attached. We don’t publish those, and any vendor who does is guessing about your close rate, your cycle length and your hit rate on new leads.

Your actual savings. Two teams with identical headcount can have opposite outcomes from the same automation, because the variable is how much of the day went into chores. That’s a measurement you take in your own business, not one we can hand you.

Four AI teammates, specifically

Four roles map to the chores that crowd out selling time. Keep them as a checklist for the third box in Part 2, not as a pitch.

Mercury (Shenxing, sales companion) meeting recordings become structured notes, follow-up emails arrive drafted, meeting prep lands the night before, lead scoring and five-dimension deal diagnosis run in the same pass.

Scout (Baixiaosheng, research) outreach drafted from an account profile with a compliance pass, new leads scored on arrival, company domains resolved to named contacts.

Medic (Pingyizhi, deal doctor) five-dimension diagnosis on stuck deals, hourly anomaly sweep across the pipeline, a daily health score, and a line from published content through to the closed deal.

Daedalus (Huang Yaoshi, builder) conversational setup: describe the role you want, tick the data it may read (read-only), attach the skills, and it configures the workflow for you.

Above them sits the lead teammate, SILI, which keeps the memory and hands you a one-screen morning brief: pipeline, risks, and the decisions waiting on you.

Three things you can do this week

Count your seats. Open your CRM billing page and write down per-seat price × seats. That is the first row of your table, and it does not change when you hire.

Pull your two benchmark rows. One from the US column, one from the China column, and write the source and date next to each. Benchmarks without dates are anecdotes.

List the chores you want gone. Write down three tasks you’d hand over tomorrow if you could: the weekly report, the follow-up drafts, the post-show clean-up. Then check whether any of them actually need a human.

That list is the only part of this exercise where more information beats more analysis. If two of your three chores are drafting and summarising, the arithmetic will look one way. If all three are negotiation and relationship work, it will look another, and no template can decide that for you.

The short version

Cost comparisons between people and software go wrong in two directions: pretending the software replaces the person, or pretending the person’s cost is only their salary. Neither survives contact with a real P&L. Take the benchmarks, fill in your own numbers, leave the last row of the table where it is: the AI does the chores, you make the calls.
