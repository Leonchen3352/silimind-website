The Estimate You Approved Three Months Ago: Where AI Quotes and Actual Billing Diverge

November 22, 2026 SiliMind

The day the bill arrived, nobody could say where the money went

(The scene below is a composite, assembled from the reconciliation situations companies with AI features run into all the time. It is not a record of any real company; people and figures are illustrative.)

An eighty-person company shipped an AI chat feature. The first users loved it — no more opening browsers and scrolling line by line. The second month's bill arrived at double the approved budget. The boss called the IT lead in and asked where the money went. The IT lead pulled up the estimate sheet from three months earlier: "approximately ¥0.02 per call." The number looked tiny.

I have seen this scene a few times, and IT's account is always the same: the sheet was calculated from the vendor's unit price, and the unit price was indeed correct. What stalls everything has never been the unit-price column.

The problem is not that ¥0.02. That month, the feature was called 120,000 times.

In the estimate sheet, the "number of calls" column was empty.

The cost of an AI feature never lives in the "how much per call" column. It lives in three others: who will call it, how many times they can call, and when it stops. Fill the unit-price column as accurately as you like — it will not survive an empty calls column.

One call actually carries three numbers

Most vendors quote you the first one. A single call carries three numbers at once.

NameWhat it isWho sets itWhen it appearsWhat goes wrong
EstimatePer-call price converted from the upstream official list priceUpstream pricing; we surface it in the interfaceBefore the call, shown in the UI and the answerYou budget against it, but it was only ever an estimate
SettlementThe money the upstream actually collectedThe upstream providerWhen the billing cycle closesYour number and theirs don't match — and you find out a month late
Budget capThe most this feature may spend this monthYou, or your adminSet in advanceIf nobody sets it, there isn't one

Estimates are for the person deciding; settlements are for finance reconciling; budget caps are for whoever holds the purse. The most common consequence of mixing them: treating the estimate as the settlement. The month-end bill runs over budget, the first reaction is "the math is wrong" — when really an estimate and a settlement were never the same thing.

Subscriptions and per-call pricing are two different cost structures. Why AI sales tools fit poorly with China-based teams covered the subscription side; this piece is about managing the per-call side.

One detail worth writing down: these per-call APIs usually don't charge for empty results. One query returning nothing equals zero spend. That barely moves a budget, but it is a sharp way to tell whether a feature is being wastefully re-called.

The quote must appear at the moment of "should I call this"

Many products put the price after the result comes back — one line: "This query cost ¥0.003." By then the money is spent, and the user is holding an after-the-fact bill. What actually works is three layers, each blocking a different mistake.

Layer one sits before the decision. When the model decides whether to call a tool, the tool's description already carries the price, plus an instruction to report the cost to the user afterwards. This layer blocks "should this have been called at all." The user asks "how is this content performing," the model reaches for a social-data query — whether that call is worth saving should be priced before the question is even asked.

Layer two sits at the moment of the call. The instant it fires, the interface shows a price note, without waiting for the result. This layer blocks "it is spending right now."

Layer three sits in the result. The tool result returns with a cost note folded into the final answer. This layer blocks "you find out after the money is gone."

The three layers guard three different things: whether to call, that it is spending, and knowing afterwards. Remove any one, and every version of the money the user sees is incomplete.

We fixed the division of labor in one sentence: the cap is a number a human sets, the notice is words a human reads, and the AI's only job is to say the price before the question leaves your mouth.

Estimated from official list prices, the per-call costs of a few common channels run roughly: Instagram and TikTok content search about $0.0035 per call, YouTube about $0.0029, TikTok single-post lookup by numeric ID about $0.0005, Chinese keyword research about ¥0.003. These are estimates from upstream providers' official list prices; we quote them as-is, empty results are free, and they are not settlement prices. To say it plainly: this quoting has never run through real billing, so it can only be an estimate — do not reconcile against it.

Hard caps belong to the tenant, not to a person

A cap is the backstop that stops one person from burning the whole budget. Two design decisions make it actually work.

First: which layer does it attach to? We designed the paid-data-source call cap to attach at the tenant level, not to any individual user. The reason is practical: people leave, switch roles, lend accounts. Cap by person, and every personnel change makes the accounting permanently ambiguous. A tenant-level cap corresponds to one budget, and one budget has exactly one definition.

Second: does it have a default? It must. We ship a conservative default daily call cap that an admin can raise. A cap without a default equals no cap, because "let's not set one, we'll deal with it if something happens" is a step that never comes. The default's job is not to block anyone — it is to make sure that the first time something goes wrong, you already know how the money flowed out.

429 is not an error; it is a sentence

429 in HTTP reads as "too many requests," and it is a normal business state. But plenty of products throw it up as a red error box. A red box tells the user "the system is broken" — and the next move is either a screenshot to the group chat, or quietly never using the feature again.

When the quota runs out, the interface should show four things:

  1. One plain sentence: today's quota is used up; it resets tomorrow. Not "requests too frequent."
  2. When it resets. Daily or hourly, the restore time should be visible, so people know how long to wait.
  3. Who to ask. Who can raise the cap, and how. Only admins can change this number — so tell the ordinary user who to find.
  4. What actually happened to the last call. Did it fail, or succeed and get rejected? That sentence decides whether the user will retry.

Six questions to ask a vendor before signing

Ask them in order. The question they fumble is the one you will fight about later.

A budget you cannot compute precisely is acceptable. A budget where "when the math is wrong, nobody can ever tell" is not.

Before your next signature, pull out that estimate sheet and fill in the "number of calls" column. The unit price is theirs to state; the call count is yours to estimate.
